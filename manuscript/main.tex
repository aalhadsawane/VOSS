\title{VOSS: Scheduling Attested Session State for Serverless Computing on Volunteer Machines}

\author{Aalhad Sawane}
\affiliation{\institution{\todo{institution}}\country{\todo{country}}}
\email{aalhadsawane@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
Short serverless functions spend most of their time waiting for a sandbox to start or for packets to cross a long distance,
while machines close to users sit idle. We ask whether those idle consumer machines can serve latency-sensitive requests.
Two things make this hard. Owners switch machines off without warning, which destroys every warm sandbox on the host.
And owners cannot be trusted: they can read the code and data they run.
We observe that remote attestation, in which a machine proves what software it booted, turns a hostile owner into an
unreliable one. The owner can still stop or delay the machine, but can no longer read or forge anything.
What remains is a scheduling problem over \emph{attested session state}: proof of trust, secret keys and warm sandboxes
are slow to create, vanish when a session ends, and widen the attack surface with every host that holds them.
We present \voss{}, a scheduler built around this problem. A slow loop plans where keys and warm state live, with spare
capacity for lost hosts. A fast loop places each request and duplicates risky ones. Failures are detected from missing
progress rather than missing heartbeats, and schedulers are replaced without consensus among volunteers. Workers
reach their scheduler through a provider-hosted publish/subscribe broker, because volunteer addresses are neither stable nor reachable.
In a deterministic simulator calibrated on a TPM-equipped machine, \voss{} meets a 3\,s tail-latency target in
\ViabAPThreeVoss{} of \ViabACells{} churn-and-adversary settings and \ViabBPThreeVoss{} of \ViabBCells{} substrate-and-load settings,
where the strongest simple baseline meets it in none. Several designed components do not pay off, and we report those too.
\end{abstract}
