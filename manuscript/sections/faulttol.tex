\section{Fault Tolerance and Accountability}
\label{sec:ft}

By Property 1, one set of mechanisms must handle crashes, churn and malice. This section describes how failures are detected, how schedulers are replaced
without consensus among volunteers, and how work is accounted.

\subsection{Fault model and the progress principle}
Table~\ref{tab:faults-model} lists the failures the mechanisms must handle. The hard cases are those where \emph{liveness signals look healthy}: a
black-holing host keeps sending heartbeats while dropping work, an on--off host builds a clean record before defecting, and a drain notice may be a lie. We therefore adopt the
principle that \emph{liveness signals from untrusted hosts are hints, and progress observed by trusted or attested parties is evidence}. The trusted observers are the routers; the attested
observers are the worker agents and the scheduler code, whose \emph{hosts} are still untrusted for availability.

\subsection{Progress-based failure detection}
\label{sec:detection}
Table~\ref{tab:detectors} lists the detectors. Each watches an event that must occur if the component is working and attributes a missing event to a suspect.
The router's \emph{dispatch watchdog} suspects a scheduler (or its cluster) when an enqueued invocation is not acknowledged within $T_{\text{dispatch}}$. The scheduler's \emph{result watchdog} suspects a worker
that has accepted work but not returned a result by the predicted completion quantile. A \emph{cluster-stall} report from routers and a \emph{decision-latency SLO} on the scheduler's attested timestamps
catch a scheduler that is alive but slow. The \emph{outcome-rate} model (Section~\ref{sec:estimators}) catches selective and on--off behavior. Heartbeat and accrual detectors \cite{hayashibara2004phi} are kept only as a fast
path for crashes and are never sufficient alone. A suspected worker receives no new work, its in-flight work is hedged or retried, and it loses provisioned eligibility; a suspected scheduler triggers failover.

\subsection{Scheduler failover without volunteer consensus}
\label{sec:succession}
Replicated state machines among volunteers would be slow and would reintroduce an agreement problem on hosts that may be adversarial. \voss{} avoids it because the provider-operated orchestrator is a
reliable arbiter, and a scheduler's state is \emph{soft}. Fig.~\ref{fig:failover} shows the sequence.

\textbf{Appointment and fencing.} The orchestrator appoints a scheduler by an atomic compare-and-swap on the cluster's \emph{epoch} in its database, in the manner of a lease
\cite{burrows2006chubby}. The appointment token binds the cluster, the epoch and the \emph{session-certificate identifier} of the appointee, so a cloned appliance with the same image but a different
session key is not appointed. Workers accept dispatches only from the current (epoch, certificate) pair and reject lower epochs with \texttt{STALE}, which acts as a fencing token.
A scheduler that cannot reach the orchestrator for a lease period stops dispatching on its own, and workers fence it anyway. A partitioned scheduler can therefore cause only duplicate work, which is within at-least-once semantics.

\textbf{Soft state.} A scheduler keeps no durable state on its host (Table~\ref{tab:soft}). Undispatched invocations are held by routers, running work and confirmed provisioning by workers, learned estimator state in a control-plane checkpoint
refreshed every few tens of seconds, and work records in receipts. After appointment, the successor installs the epoch and performs a \emph{roll call}: it asks workers for their running and queued work, confirmed pins and keys, retained outcomes
and cache inventory, rebuilds its view, replays outcomes, and re-plans from the checkpoint. Because nothing authoritative lives on the old host, a rolled-back or restarted appliance is a new instance that must be re-appointed, and its stale memory is never trusted.

\textbf{Succession list.} The orchestrator keeps a ranked list of candidates by survival, speed, centrality and assurance, with failure-domain diversity and hysteresis. Every attested worker is a candidate, because the scheduler role is in the image, so promotion costs no boot;
a warm standby also receives heartbeats. Planned handovers on a drain notice or decision-latency degradation flush a checkpoint before the swap; a \emph{fake} drain by a malicious scheduler host just causes a clean handover.

\subsection{Worker failures}
Session end discards keys and warm sandboxes; the invocation is retried or hedged elsewhere, preferring provisioned hosts. A per-cluster retry token bucket and router spillover contain retry storms during regional
waves. Drain and preemption notices from the owner side only make the scheduler more conservative (stop new dispatch, shorten predicted life) and never excuse an omission in the estimators. A change point or a watchdog streak
marks a host suspected, revokes its eligibility and triggers re-planning.

\subsection{Attested receipts and credits}
\label{sec:receipts}
Each result carries a receipt signed with the session key: invocation and attempt identifiers, worker and certificate identifiers, epoch, execution time, \emph{reference work units}, and a digest of the output. The router verifies it against the
certificate (and the verifier's revocation list) and appends it to the operational database. A host cannot claim work it did not do; it can only decline to do work, which is an omission handled above. Credits are
proportional to reference work units, not elapsed time, so throttling earns nothing, and a small credit rewards cache pinned by the plan while the host answered probes. Reputation is kept apart from the survival and omission estimators, which
have disjoint inputs (session ends versus mid-session omissions), so that no signal is counted twice; it aggregates declared-commitment behavior over the long term and is used for rewards and as a placement tie-breaker only. We deliberately omit a
blockchain: receipts are already verifiable by third parties, and the only remaining risk is the control plane withholding records.
