\section{Reaching a Scheduler on a Volunteer Machine}
\label{sec:network}
\label{sec:broker}
\label{sec:placement}

In the clustered architecture, routers must send invocations to a scheduler that runs on a volunteer machine, and the
scheduler must send assignments to its workers. Home routers perform network address translation: a machine behind one can
open connections outwards but cannot accept connections from outside, and its public address changes when the router
restarts. HTTP and gRPC assume a server at an address its clients know, so every scheduler replacement would force every
router and worker to learn a new address at once; hole punching works only for some pairs of home routers. With hundreds of
clusters whose schedulers move between machines, neither scales.

We therefore use a publish/subscribe broker run by the provider at a stable address. Every party opens one outward
connection to it. Each cluster has its own topics, named by the cluster's number: the scheduler subscribes to the cluster's
scheduler topic, workers to their own topics, and routers publish invocations to the scheduler topic. Nobody needs another
party's address, and replacing a scheduler means only that the successor subscribes to the same topic. We take
MQTT~\cite{mqtt5} as the protocol, whose clients always connect outwards and whose at-least-once delivery suits messages
that are idempotent or carry the scheduler's epoch. MQTT gives no protection against the broker itself~\cite{buccafurri2024mqtti},
so each connection is mutual TLS and every control message is signed end to end with the sender's session key and carries
the epoch and a sequence number; workers accept an assignment only from the scheduler appointed for the current epoch. The
broker can drop or delay messages, faults the detectors of Section~\ref{sec:ft} already handle, but cannot forge or replay
them. The costs are one extra hop and one more replicated provider service; traffic is about 0.46\,KB/s per worker.

Compute is not why the scheduler runs on a volunteer machine: on provider hardware, greedy planning for $10^5$ workers in
clusters of 30 needs about one processor core. The global architecture avoids the broker, the extra hop and a malicious
scheduler host altogether; we keep the clustered one because it needs no per-cluster provider server and because it is the
harder case for fault tolerance. The simulator models only the delay of messages (a geographic delay plus 20\,ms per relayed
end), not broker capacity, reconnection or the cost of signatures.
