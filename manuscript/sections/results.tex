\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organise the results around the questions the design raises. Appendix~\ref{app:score} lists every hypothesis with its verdict.

\subsection{What does trust cost, and how does it shape start-up?}
\label{sec:res-cs1}
The appliance pays a large one-off cost for each session, \SimApplSession\,s with our pre-measurement parameters and \CalAttested\,s from power-on on the calibration machine. A confidential VM needs \SimCvmSession\,s and a confidential-container pool \SimCocoSession\,s.
We had expected confidential VMs to be 10 to 100 times \emph{slower} at establishing trust. That was refuted: they are about 93\% faster, because the appliance's cost is a reboot.
Once the session exists, though, the appliance is as fast as an unattested Firecracker host: warm and snapshot starts are within a few percent of the unattested figures (H5, supported), because attestation is paid per session, not per invocation.

\subsection{Where does volunteer serverless work at all?}
\label{sec:res-viab}
Averages hide the regimes in which a design fails, so we map feasibility. Figure~\ref{fig:viab} colours each cell by which of \voss{} and the best simple baseline is feasible (success at least 0.99 and 95\% of deadlines met), and prints \voss{}'s P99.

\voss{} is the only feasible system in all \ViabACells{} cells of panel A, which spans three churn levels and up to 20\% omitting owners, in \ViabBMetOnly{} of \ViabBCells{} cells of panel B and in \ViabCMetOnly{} of \ViabCCells{} cells of panel C.
With an absolute P99 target of 3\,s it is feasible in \ViabAPThreeVoss{} of \ViabACells{}, \ViabBPThreeVoss{} of \ViabBCells{} and \ViabCPThreeVoss{} of \ViabCCells{} cells, and the simple baseline in none. At 5\,s, \voss{} is feasible in \ViabAPFiveVoss{}, \ViabBPFiveVoss{} and \ViabCPFiveVoss{} cells.

The map also shows where \voss{} is the wrong choice. On confidential-VM substrates at higher load, the simple baseline is the only feasible system in \ViabBMetBase{} cells. We conjecture that with cheap session set-up there is little cold-start cost left for planning to remove, but we did not test this.
With 100 workers no system is feasible in any cell of panel C, so the design needs a minimum population, and at a deadline-met target of 0.98 no system is feasible anywhere.

\subsection{Does planning attested session state pay?}
\label{sec:res-plan}
This is the central claim, so we compare planners on identical scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:plan}). The greedy planner lowers synchronous P99 by \GreedyNoneMed\% at medium churn and \GreedyNoneHigh\% at high churn against no planning, and by \GreedyKeepMed\% and \GreedyKeepHigh\% against a keep-alive timer.
The exact planner gives \HOnePctNoneMed\% and \HOnePctNoneHigh\% against no planning, and \HOnePctKeepMed\% and \HOnePctKeepHigh\% against keep-alive (H1, supported). Planning costs donor memory: the exact planner holds about \MilpWarmRatio{} times the warm memory of keep-alive at medium churn.

The extra gain from the exact solver is small and not established. At medium churn it improves P99 by \MilpGreedyMed\% over the greedy planner, \MilpGreedyMedBelow{} our 5\% threshold. At high churn its point estimate is \MilpGreedyHigh\%, \MilpGreedyHighBelow{} the threshold, but the 95\% interval runs from a \MilpGreedyHighBest\% reduction to a \MilpGreedyHighWorst\% increase and includes zero (H8, inconclusive). Ten seeds can neither confirm that the exact solver matters nor show that it does not.
Its solve takes \MilpSolveS\,s per cluster against \GreedySolveMs\,ms, yet the simulated clock charges both about 0.1\,s. Our rule required the gain to be below 5\% in every regime, and the data do not show that at high churn, so we do not claim the rule is met. We keep greedy as the default, a choice made before these runs.

To see which components matter, Figure~\ref{fig:abl} removes one at a time (10\% omitting owners). Removing lifecycle-aware placement costs \AblLifecycleP\,s of P99, removing planning \AblPlanningP\,s, removing robust coverage \AblRobustP\,s and removing hedging \AblHedgeP\,s.
The components that buy P99, planning and robustness, also raise exposure: without planning, total exposure is lower by \AblPlanningExp{} units (Eq.~\ref{eq:exposure}), because fewer hosts hold keys. Geo-clustering is the only component whose removal \emph{improves} P99 (by \AblGeoP\,s) and also cuts exposure (by \AblGeoExp{} units). We return to this in Section~\ref{sec:res-nopay}.

\subsection{Does progress-based detection make malice look like churn?}
\label{sec:res-ft}
\textbf{Scheduler faults.} Table~\ref{tab:faults} injects crashes, partitions, black holes and slowdowns into scheduler hosts. Heartbeats alone cannot see a black hole, so detection waits for a 60\,s timeout (\BhDetectHb\,s) and the run suffers \BhTimeoutsHb{} timeouts, against \BhDetectProg\,s and \BhTimeoutsProg{} with progress-based detection (H-FT1, supported).

\textbf{Hedging.} Always duplicating cuts P99 but wastes \AlwaysWastePct\% extra attempts. Risk-proportional hedging wastes \RpWastePct\%, and its P99 is \HedgePNinetyninePct\% lower at $\alpha=0.1$, with an interval from \HedgePNinetynineLo\% to $+$\HedgePNinetynineHi\% (H-FT3, inconclusive). \subsection{What do robustness and exposure cost?}
\label{sec:res-pareto}
Robustness $f$ and the exposure weight trade latency against key exposure (E23). Exposure grows with $f$ (\ExpFZero, \ExpFOne{} and \ExpFTwo{} at $f=0$, $0.1$ and $0.2$), as expected, since covering any $f$ failures needs more key-holding hosts.
Aggregate P99 stays within \ParetoPNinetynineLo--\ParetoPNinetynineHi\,s across configurations, so the knee at $f=\ParetoKneeF$ saves \ParetoKneeExpSaved\% of exposure against the best-latency point for \ParetoKneePNinetynineLoss\% more P99 (H3, supported).
Per tenant class the picture is less benign. Low-sensitivity tenants stay at about \ParetoLowMax\,s or below, but for high-sensitivity tenants P99 reaches \ParetoHighMax\,s in one robust configuration. The aggregate knee should not be applied to every tenant class, and each needs its own operating point.

\subsection{Which components do not earn their complexity?}
\label{sec:res-nopay}
\textbf{Geo-clustering.} We compare one global scheduler with static regions, static $k$-means and dynamic clusters on 600 workers (about 225 of them attested) at 20 invocations per second, with clusters of about 20 to 35 attested hosts. The global scheduler plans over at most 40 hosts, which is part of why it holds fewer keys, and nothing in this load stresses one scheduler's capacity.
Dynamic clusters (E05) have \emph{higher} P99 than the global scheduler by \HSevenEu\% in Europe, \HSevenEuNa\% in Europe and North America and \HSevenWorld\% worldwide (H7, refuted in all three), and hold \GeoExpRatio{} times the exposure in Europe, since each of the \GeoClusters{} clusters must cover demand with its own key holders.
We attribute this to fewer hosts per cluster for pooling warm state and keys, but did not isolate the mechanism. We found no population in which geo-clustering wins at this scale.

\textbf{Routing, caching, reputation.} Without flash crowds, nearest-region routing meets \RouteNearMet{} of deadlines and least-loaded routing \RouteLeastMet{}, because it ignores distance.
Cost-aware caching with peer fetch and a disk tier is at most \HSixPct\% better than plain LRU in P99, and the intervals include zero (H6, inconclusive); all variants start about 98\% of invocations from a warm sandbox, which leaves little for disk tiers to improve.
Receipts stay off the critical path (H10, supported): lengthening settlement from 10 to 300\,s changes P99 by at most $\pm$\HTenPct\%, and the stream is small, about \RecvRows{} rows and \RecvKBs\,KB per second at 20 invocations per second. Reputation adds little (\RepPNinetyninePct\% lower P99, weak), and credit is highly concentrated (Gini \RepGini; the top 10\% of workers earn \RepTopTen\% of credit and \RepStarved\% earn almost none), which is a fairness problem for an incentive that relies on credits.

\subsection{How does \voss{} compare with baselines and a commercial reference?}
\label{sec:res-simple}
\textbf{Simple baseline.} On a homogeneous, stable, benign pool we expected the simple baseline to match \voss{} (E10). It does not: \voss{} has \HElevenPct\% lower P99 (H11, refuted), which we attribute to planning's pre-warming against churn.
The baseline holds about half of \voss{}'s key exposure, though (\voss{} is \SimpleExpRatio{} times higher). Whether the latency is worth the exposure is a policy choice that the trade-off front of the previous section lets a deployer make.

\textbf{Commercial reference.} On the same workload at list prices (E17), \voss{}'s P99 at 20 invocations per second is \CommPNinetyninePct\% and its median \CommPFiftyPct\% above the Lambda-like reference. Cost per million invocations, including the control plane and excluding volunteer credits, falls from \CommCostTen{} USD at 10 invocations per second to \CommCostTwenty{} at 20 and \CommCostForty{} at 40 as the fixed tier is amortised, against about \CommCostRef{} USD for the references.
\voss{} is cost-competitive only above roughly 20 invocations per second on this workload and never has lower latency.

\textbf{Scale.} We grow the population from $10^2$ to $10^5$ workers at constant load per worker in a cheaper mode (coarse heartbeats, greedy planning, two simulated minutes, E18). Up to $10^4$ workers success is \ScaleTenKSucc\%. At $3\times10^4$ it falls to \ScaleMidSucc\% with a decision P99 of \ScaleMidDecide\,s, and at $10^5$ to \ScaleBigSucc\% with a synchronous P99 of \ScaleBigPNinetynine\,s and a decision P99 of \ScaleBigDecide\,s.
Decision latency grows from about 0.1\,s to tens of seconds, while control traffic per worker stays flat (\ScaleMsgLo--\ScaleMsgHi{} messages per second), so the limit lies in the central decision path and not in message volume. We did not isolate which component saturates first, and the two largest points use three seeds.

\subsection{Do the conclusions depend on our calibration?}
\label{sec:res-calib}
We repeated the key comparison under the placeholder and laptop-measured bundles (E24). \voss{}'s P99 is \CalSensVossPNinetynineMeas\,s with measured parameters against \CalSensVossPNinetyninePlc\,s with placeholders, while the simple baseline moves from \CalSensSimplePNinetyninePlc\,s to \CalSensSimplePNinetynineMeas\,s.
The advantage of planning therefore grows under measured parameters. Absolute values should not be read as predictions.

\subsection{Summary of verdicts}
\label{sec:res-score}
Of the hypotheses stated before the runs, \ScoreSupported{} are supported, \ScoreWeak{} weak, \ScoreInconclusive{} inconclusive and \ScoreRefuted{} refuted. The refuted ones (geo-clustering, the simple baseline on homogeneous hosts, the cost ratio of confidential VMs) and the inconclusive ones (the robust constraint for success, the exact solver, caching, hedging) are as informative as the supported ones.
