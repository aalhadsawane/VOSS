\subsection{Is the planner needed?}
We compare the planner with the strongest single-loop alternatives on 200 workers for 30 minutes, with 20 paired seeds at each
of medium and high churn (E04b; 10 seeds for the exact program). All systems use the same placement. Table~\ref{tab:front}
and Figure~\ref{fig:front} give P99, warm memory and key exposure. Success is above 99.9\% for every system and every system
meets a 3\,s P99 target, so the differences are in the tail between 1.2 and 1.8\,s.

The greedy planner has \RfourDecompGreedyMed\% (medium churn) and \RfourDecompGreedyHigh\% (high churn) lower P99 than the best single loop,
which is a fixed or unbounded keep-alive timer (paired ratio \RfourHtwelveMedRatio{} with 95\% interval [\RfourHtwelveMedLo, \RfourHtwelveMedHi]
at medium churn and \RfourHtwelveHighRatio{} [\RfourHtwelveHighLo, \RfourHtwelveHighHi] at high churn, below our 0.95 threshold in \RfourHtwelveMedBelow{} and
\RfourHtwelveHighBelow{} of 20 seeds; H12 supported). The hybrid histogram policy of Azure Functions, which learns each sandbox's idle
times, holds a third less warm memory than a fixed timer and is no faster. The price of the planner is exposure: it
holds keys on about 2.5 times as many workers as the single loops (\RfourExpPlanGreedyMed{} against \RfourExpKeepaliveTtlMed{} at medium churn). The
exact program is not better than greedy (P99 \RfourPPlanMilpMed\,s against \RfourPPlanGreedyMed\,s at medium churn, \RfourPPlanMilpHigh\,s against \RfourPPlanGreedyHigh\,s at
high churn) while holding about 1.5 times the warm memory, and its solve time is not charged to the simulated clock
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), which favours it.

What part of this is planning, and what is merely holding keys? We compared against a baseline that holds each tenant's key
ahead of demand on the most reliable workers, in the number the greedy planner would use for that tenant but with no robust
cover and no warm-state planning (\emph{provisioned\_keys}). It has the same exposure as greedy and P99 \RfourPProvisionedKeysMed\,s,
which is \RfourDecompPkMed\% below the best single loop at medium churn and \RfourDecompPkHigh\% at high churn: about \RfourDecompShareMed\% of greedy's gain.
Choosing the workers well and planning warm sandboxes adds the remaining fifth, with greedy at \RfourHfifteenMedRatio{} of
\emph{provisioned\_keys}' P99 at medium churn (interval [\RfourHfifteenMedLo, \RfourHfifteenMedHi]) and \RfourHfifteenHighRatio{} at high churn, which excludes 1 but not our 5\%
threshold (H15, weak). Most of the planner's value is therefore the set-level decision of how many workers should hold each key,
which a per-request loop cannot make, and not the finer choices of who and what.

The loss-model planner \emph{erlang\_greedy} lowers exposure by \RfourHthirteenMedExp{} and \RfourHthirteenHighExp{} of greedy's at medium and high churn, but
holds \RfourHthirteenMedWarm{} and \RfourHthirteenHighWarm{} times the warm memory and is \RfourHthirteenMedRatio{} and \RfourHthirteenHighRatio{} times as slow (H13, not supported
as a replacement). We report it as the lower-exposure point on the front. The variant that enforces an exposure budget
(\emph{erlang\_dpp}) matches its latency but did not meet its budget (H14).

\subsection{Where the gain comes from}
\label{sec:res-session}
Three checks narrow the mechanism. First, in the seeds where the planner's lead is largest, a baseline's tail is one episode
of 40--120\,s in which synchronous invocations of one tenant queue for about 10\,s behind long asynchronous jobs on the two to
six workers that hold its key, while greedy has 11--20 holders. Key-release delay accounts for 0.1--0.4\% of synchronous
invocations in every system. Second, a key that serves a whole session is amortised over many invocations, but the planner
does not keep keys long: in the same runs it releases a key \RfourRelPlanGreedyMed{} times after warm-up at medium churn, against \RfourRelKeepaliveTtlMed{}
for a keep-alive timer, so one release serves about \RfourAmortPlanGreedyMed{} synchronous invocations against about \RfourAmortKeepaliveTtlMed. It buys spare capacity
on many holders rather than a few durable ones. Third, we varied session length from a quarter of to four times the medium-churn
mean, with Weibull shape 0.6 and 1.0 (E25, 10 seeds). The planner's lead over the best single loop is between
\RfourEtwentyfiveKoneMfourA{} and \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneMoneA{} of P99 in all six cells and varies little with session length: at shape 1.0 it peaks at mean
length and exceeds the longest sessions' by \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFour{} [\RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFourLo, \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFourHi], but the same comparison to the shortest
sessions is not distinguishable from zero. Hiding survival estimates from the planner changes P99 by less than 3\% in every
cell, so we cannot attribute the gain to choosing long-lived workers (H16). Plausibly the planner's 60\,s look-ahead is short against sessions of
hours, but we did not test this.

\subsection{Ablations}
Removing one component of \voss{} at a time (10\% of owners dropping work, E19, original placement) shows that making
placement ignore start-up levels costs \AblLifecycleP\,s of P99, removing the planner \AblPlanningP\,s, removing the cover against
losing $f$ workers \AblRobustP\,s and removing duplicates \AblHedgeP\,s. The components that buy P99 also raise exposure, by
\AblPlanningExp{} units for the planner. Removing the exposure term changes P99 by \AblExposureP\,s (interval up to \AblExposurePHi\,s, which includes zero) and
raises exposure by \AblExposureExp, so limiting exposure costs no measurable latency. Geographic clustering is the only component whose
removal improves P99 (by \AblGeoP\,s); in that experiment the global scheduler also ran on a volunteer machine, which favours
clusters, so the comparison understates the global architecture. It holds \GeoExpRatio{} times the key exposure of a global scheduler with
\GeoClusters{} clusters in Europe, since each cluster needs its own key holders. At the 600-worker scale we simulate, the global scheduler was
faster by \HSevenEu\% (Europe), \HSevenEuNa\% (Europe and North America) and \HSevenWorld\% (world).

\subsection{Judging workers by returned work}
With 20\% of owners dropping work, \voss{} keeps \VossSuccAlpha\% of requests successful, while detection by heartbeats with otherwise
identical machinery falls to \HbSuccAlpha\% and loses \HbOmitRatio{} times more results. Against a scheduler that discards invocations while
sending heartbeats, detection by returned work takes \BhDetectProg\,s with \BhTimeoutsProg{} timeouts, and heartbeats alone need the 60\,s timeout
(\BhDetectHb\,s, \BhTimeoutsHb{} timeouts). A crashed scheduler is found in \CrashDetectProg\,s and replaced in \CrashFailover\,s; a slowed one takes \SlowFailover\,s
because evidence must build up before a slowdown can be told from load. Protection against losing $f$ workers changes success by only
\FzeroSuccDelta{} percentage points (it buys tail latency, not success), and duplicating only at-risk invocations is cheap insurance that we
could not show to be necessary.
