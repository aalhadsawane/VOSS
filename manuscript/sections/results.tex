\subsection{Is provisioning needed?}

of medium and high churn (E04b; 10 seeds for the exact program). All systems use the same placement. Table~\ref{tab:front}
and Figure~\ref{fig:front} give P99, warm memory and key exposure. Success is above 99.9\% for every system and every system
meets a 3\,s P99 target, so the differences are in the tail between 1.2 and 1.8\,s.

Greedy provisioning has \RfourDecompGreedyMed\% (medium churn) and \RfourDecompGreedyHigh\% (high churn) lower P99 than the best keep-alive baseline,
which is a fixed or unbounded keep-alive timer (paired ratio \RfourHtwelveMedRatio{} with 95\% interval [\RfourHtwelveMedLo, \RfourHtwelveMedHi]
at medium churn and \RfourHtwelveHighRatio{} [\RfourHtwelveHighLo, \RfourHtwelveHighHi] at high churn, below our 0.95 threshold in \RfourHtwelveMedBelow{} and
\RfourHtwelveHighBelow{} of 20 seeds; H12 supported). The hybrid histogram policy of Azure Functions, which learns each sandbox's idle
times, holds a third less warm memory than a fixed timer and is no faster. The price of provisioning is exposure: it
holds keys on about 2.5 times as many workers as the keep-alive baselines (\RfourExpPlanGreedyMed{} against \RfourExpKeepaliveTtlMed{} at medium churn). The
exact program is not better than greedy (P99 \RfourPPlanMilpMed\,s against \RfourPPlanGreedyMed\,s at medium churn, \RfourPPlanMilpHigh\,s against \RfourPPlanGreedyHigh\,s at
high churn) while holding about 1.5 times the warm memory, and its solve time is not charged to the simulated clock
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), which favours it.

What part of this is provisioning, and what is merely holding keys? We compared against a baseline that holds each tenant's key
ahead of demand on the most reliable workers (\emph{early keys}), in the number greedy provisioning would use for that tenant but with no robust
cover and no provisioning of warm sandboxes. It has the same exposure as greedy and P99 \RfourPProvisionedKeysMed\,s,
which is \RfourDecompPkMed\% below the best keep-alive baseline at medium churn and \RfourDecompPkHigh\% at high churn: about \RfourDecompShareMed\% of greedy's gain.
Choosing the workers well and provisioning warm sandboxes adds the remaining fifth, with greedy at \RfourHfifteenMedRatio{} of
the early-keys baseline's P99 at medium churn (interval [\RfourHfifteenMedLo, \RfourHfifteenMedHi]) and \RfourHfifteenHighRatio{} at high churn, which excludes 1 but not our 5\%
threshold (H15, weak). Most of the value of provisioning is therefore the set-level decision of how many workers should hold each key,
which a per-request decision cannot make, and not the finer choices of who and what.

Loss-model provisioning lowers exposure by \RfourHthirteenMedExp{} and \RfourHthirteenHighExp{} of greedy's at medium and high churn, but
holds \RfourHthirteenMedWarm{} and \RfourHthirteenHighWarm{} times the warm memory and is \RfourHthirteenMedRatio{} and \RfourHthirteenHighRatio{} times as slow (H13, not supported
as a replacement). We report it as the lower-exposure point on the front. The variant that enforces an exposure budget
matches its latency but did not meet its budget (H14).

\subsection{Where the gain comes from}
\label{sec:res-session}
Three checks narrow the mechanism. First, in the seeds where provisioning's lead is largest, a baseline's tail is one episode
of 40--120\,s in which synchronous invocations of one tenant queue for about 10\,s behind long asynchronous jobs on the two to
six workers that hold its key, while greedy has 11--20 holders. Key-release delay accounts for 0.1--0.4\% of synchronous
invocations in every system. Second, a key that serves a whole session is amortised over many invocations, but provisioning
does not keep keys long: in the same runs it releases a key \RfourRelPlanGreedyMed{} times after warm-up at medium churn, against \RfourRelKeepaliveTtlMed{}
for a keep-alive timer, so one release serves about \RfourAmortPlanGreedyMed{} synchronous invocations against about \RfourAmortKeepaliveTtlMed. It buys spare capacity
on many holders rather than a few durable ones. Third, we varied session length from a quarter of to four times the medium-churn
mean, with Weibull shape 0.6 and 1.0 (E25, 10 seeds). Provisioning's lead over the best keep-alive baseline is between
\RfourEtwentyfiveKoneMfourA{} and \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneMoneA{} of P99 in all six cells and varies little with session length: at shape 1.0 it peaks at mean
length and exceeds the longest sessions' by \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFour{} [\RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFourLo, \RfourEtwentyfiveKoneAdiffFourHi], but the same comparison to the shortest
sessions is not distinguishable from zero. Hiding survival estimates from provisioning changes P99 by less than 3\% in every
cell, so we cannot attribute the gain to choosing long-lived workers (H16). Plausibly provisioning's 60\,s look-ahead is short against sessions of
hours, but we did not test this.

\subsection{Ablations}
Removing one component of \voss{} at a time (10\% of owners dropping work, E19, original placement) shows that making
placement ignore start-up levels costs \AblLifecycleP\,s of P99, removing provisioning \AblPlanningP\,s, removing the cover against
losing $f$ workers \AblRobustP\,s and removing duplicates \AblHedgeP\,s. The components that buy P99 also raise exposure, by
\AblPlanningExp{} units for provisioning. Removing the exposure term changes P99 by \AblExposureP\,s (interval up to \AblExposurePHi\,s, which includes zero) and
raises exposure by \AblExposureExp, so limiting exposure costs no measurable latency. Geographic clustering is the only component whose
removal improves P99 (by \AblGeoP\,s); in that experiment the global scheduler also ran on a volunteer machine, which favours
clusters, so the comparison understates the global architecture. It holds \GeoExpRatio{} times the key exposure of a global scheduler with
\GeoClusters{} clusters in Europe, since each cluster needs its own key holders. At the 600-worker scale we simulate, the global scheduler was
faster by \HSevenEu\% (Europe), \HSevenEuNa\% (Europe and North America) and \HSevenWorld\% (world).

\subsection{Judging workers by returned work}
With 20\% of owners dropping work, \voss{} keeps \VossSuccAlpha\% of requests successful, while detection by heartbeats with otherwise
identical machinery falls to \HbSuccAlpha\% and loses \HbOmitRatio{} times more results. Against a scheduler that discards invocations while
sending heartbeats, detection by returned work takes \BhDetectProg\,s with \BhTimeoutsProg{} timeouts, and heartbeats alone need the 60\,s timeout
(\BhDetectHb\,s, \BhTimeoutsHb{} timeouts). A crashed scheduler is found in \CrashDetectProg\,s and replaced in \CrashFailover\,s; a slowed one takes \SlowFailover\,s
because evidence must build up before a slowdown can be told from load. Protection against losing $f$ workers changes success by only
\FzeroSuccDelta{} percentage points (it buys tail latency, not success), and duplicating only at-risk invocations is cheap insurance that we
could not show to be necessary.

\subsection{Baselines, feasibility and scale}
\textbf{Simple baseline.} The simple baseline combines static regional clusters, nearest-region routing, least-loaded placement
and keep-alive without hedging. On a homogeneous, stable pool we expected it to match \voss{} (E10b, corrected placement); it
does not. Its P99 is \RerunSimplePNinetynineHomRatio{} times \voss{}'s (95\% interval [\RerunSimplePNinetynineHomLo, \RerunSimplePNinetynineHomHi]), and \RerunSimplePNinetynineMixRatio{} times on mixed hardware
[\RerunSimplePNinetynineMixLo, \RerunSimplePNinetynineMixHi]. \voss{} holds about \RerunSimpleExpHomRatio{} and \RerunSimpleExpMixRatio{} times the key exposure. Whether the latency is worth the exposure is a policy
choice, which Figure~\ref{fig:front} lets a deployer make. Calibration changes the size of the gap but not its sign: with
costs measured on the laptop \voss{} reaches P99 \RerunCalSensVossPNinetynineMeas\,s against \RerunCalSensVossPNinetyninePlc\,s with placeholders, while the simple baseline
moves from \RerunCalSensSimplePNinetyninePlc\,s to \RerunCalSensSimplePNinetynineMeas\,s (E24b).

\textbf{Commercial-style references.} Datacenter hosts that are always on, with per-function keep-alive and nearest-region
routing, are faster (E17b). At 20 invocations per second \voss{}'s P99 is \RerunCommPNinetynineLambdaTwentyDiff\,s above the Lambda-like reference's and
\RerunCommPNinetynineAzureTwentyDiff\,s above the Azure-like one, and at 40 per second \RerunCommPNinetynineLambdaFortyDiff\,s and \RerunCommPNinetynineAzureFortyDiff\,s; at 10 per second the gap to the Azure-like
reference is not distinguishable from zero. Median latency is about 0.13\,s higher at every load. \potrem{cost analysis; removed from the abstract}
Cost per million invocations,
counting the control plane and not volunteer credits, falls from \RerunCommCostTen{} USD at 10 invocations per second to \RerunCommCostTwenty{} at 20 and
\RerunCommCostForty{} at 40, against about \RerunCommCostRefLambda{} USD for the references, so \voss{} is cheaper from about 20 invocations per second upward.

\textbf{Feasibility.} We crossed three churn levels with three adversary settings (none, and 10\% and 20\% of owners dropping or
delaying work), giving \RerunViabCells{} cells with 10 seeds each (E20ab, corrected placement), and compared \voss{} with the simple
baseline of the previous paragraph. A system is feasible in a cell by the definition in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
\voss{} is feasible in \RerunViabPTwoVoss{} of \RerunViabCells{} cells at P99 targets of 2\,s, 3\,s and 5\,s, and in \RerunViabMetVoss{} of \RerunViabCells{} when the
criterion is the share of deadlines met; its mean P99 per cell lies between \RerunViabPNinetynineLoVoss{} and \RerunViabPNinetynineHiVoss\,s. The simple baseline is feasible in
\RerunViabPFiveSimple{} cells under every criterion, with P99 between \RerunViabPNinetynineLoSimple{} and \RerunViabPNinetynineHiSimple\,s even in the cells without adversaries
(Table~\ref{tab:viab}), while its success stays above \RerunViabSuccLoSimple\%. This gap does not show that provisioning matters: the
baseline differs from \voss{} in its clusters, routing, placement and hedging as well as in keep-alive, and the keep-alive
baselines of the first subsection, which share \voss{}'s placement, all met 3\,s.

\textbf{Scale.}
Scalability (E18, original placement): success stays at \ScaleTenKSucc\% up to $10^4$ workers and falls to \ScaleMidSucc\% at $3\times10^4$ and \ScaleBigSucc\% at $10^5$,
where decision latency has grown from about 0.1\,s to tens of seconds; control traffic per worker stays flat, so the limit is the central decision path.
