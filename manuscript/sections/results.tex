\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the results around the questions the design raises. Table~\ref{tab:score} at the end lists every hypothesis with its verdict.
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] What does establishing trust cost on each substrate, and how does that shape start-up?
\item[RQ2] Where is volunteer FaaS feasible, and where is it not?
\item[RQ3] Does planning attested session state pay for itself?
\item[RQ4] Does progress-based detection make malicious owners look like churn?
\item[RQ5] What do robustness and exposure cost?
\item[RQ6] Which designed components do not earn their complexity?
\item[RQ7] How does \voss{} compare with simple baselines and a commercial reference, and how does it scale?
\end{description}

\subsection{RQ1: Cost of establishing trust across substrates}
\label{sec:res-cs1}
Table~\ref{tab:cs1} gives, for each substrate, the simulated time to establish a session and the start-up latency from each tier. Two observations shape everything that follows.
First, the appliance pays a large one-off cost per session (\SimApplSession\,s with the pre-measurement parameters; \CalAttested\,s measured end to end on the calibration machine), compared with
\SimCvmSession\,s for a confidential VM and \SimCocoSession\,s for a confidential-container pool, and loses all session state when the session ends. Second, once the session exists the appliance is as fast as an
unattested Firecracker host: warm and snapshot starts are within a few percent of the unattested figures (H5, supported), because attestation is paid per session, not per invocation. Confidential VMs invert the trade, with cheap sessions but slower
lower tiers. Our expectation that confidential-VM classes establish trust 10--100$\times$ \emph{slower} than the appliance was refuted: they are about 93\% faster, since the appliance's cost is a reboot.

\voss{} is the \emph{only} feasible system in all \ViabACells{} cells of panel A, which spans three churn regimes and up to 20\% omitting owners, in \ViabBMetOnly{} of \ViabBCells{} cells of panel B and in
\ViabCMetOnly{} of \ViabCCells{} cells of panel C. With an absolute sync P99 target of 3\,s it is feasible in \ViabAPThreeVoss{} of \ViabACells, \ViabBPThreeVoss{} of \ViabBCells{} and \ViabCPThreeVoss{} of \ViabCCells{} cells, and the simple baseline in none; at 5\,s
\voss{} is feasible in \ViabAPFiveVoss, \ViabBPFiveVoss{} and \ViabCPFiveVoss{} cells. The two criteria measure different things (a deadline is a multiple of warm latency, the absolute target is what a user perceives), which is why a baseline can pass one and fail the other.

The map also shows where \voss{} is \emph{not} the right choice. On confidential-VM and confidential-container substrates at higher load the simple baseline is the only feasible system in \ViabBMetBase{} cells
(Fig.~\ref{fig:viab}b). We conjecture that with cheap session establishment there is little cold-start cost left for planning to remove while it still adds exposure and memory; we did not test this. With 100 workers no system is feasible in any
cell of panel C (\ViabCMetNoneFive{} cells), so the design needs a minimum population. At a deadline-met target of 0.98 \emph{no} system is feasible in any cell: the ``98\% of synchronous invocations within $5\times$ warm latency'' service level is out of reach in this setting for \voss{} and the baselines alike.

\subsection{RQ3: Does planning attested session state pay?}
\label{sec:res-plan}
Table~\ref{tab:plan} compares planners on identical scenarios under medium and high churn. Planning is one of the two largest levers (with lifecycle-aware placement, Fig.~\ref{fig:abl}). The greedy planner lowers sync P99 by \GreedyNoneMed\% (medium churn) and \GreedyNoneHigh\% (high churn) against no planning, and by \GreedyKeepMed\% and \GreedyKeepHigh\%
against a keep-alive TTL; the exact MILP gives \HOnePctNoneMed\% and \HOnePctNoneHigh\% against no planning and \HOnePctKeepMed\% and \HOnePctKeepHigh\% against keep-alive (H1, supported). Planning costs donor memory: the MILP holds about \MilpWarmRatio$\times$ the warm memory of keep-alive at medium churn.
The gain from the exact solver is small. The MILP improves P99 by only \MilpGreedyMed\% (medium churn) and \HEightPct\% (high churn) over the greedy planner (H8; the interval includes zero), below our 5\% threshold, while its solve takes \MilpSolveS\,s per cluster against \GreedySolveMs\,ms. By our pre-set rule the greedy planner is therefore the method and the MILP a reference solver. What the formulation provides
beyond latency is the robust coverage condition, whose value is examined in RQ4.

Fig.~\ref{fig:abl} removes one component of \voss{} at a time (E19, 10\% omitting owners). Removing lifecycle-aware placement costs \AblLifecycleP\,s of sync P99, removing planning \AblPlanningP\,s (and lowers exposure by \AblPlanningExp, because less is pre-warmed and fewer hosts hold keys), removing robust coverage \AblRobustP\,s (exposure \AblRobustExp{} lower) and

Geo-clustering is the only component whose removal \emph{improves} P99 (\AblGeoP\,s) and cuts exposure (by \AblGeoExp), as Section~\ref{sec:res-nopay} discusses.

\subsection{RQ4: Does progress-based detection make malice look like churn?}
\label{sec:res-ft}
\textbf{Scheduler faults.} Table~\ref{tab:faults} injects crashes, partitions, black-holing and slowed scheduler hosts. Heartbeats alone cannot see a black hole: the host keeps reporting liveness while dropping work, so detection waits for the 60\,s timeout
(\BhDetectHb\,s) and the run suffers \BhTimeoutsHb{} timeouts, against \BhDetectProg\,s and \BhTimeoutsProg{} with progress-based detection (H-FT1, supported). For crashes, progress detection needs \CrashDetectProg\,s and failover \CrashFailover\,s. A slowed but live scheduler is the hardest case: failover takes
\SlowFailover\,s because progress evidence must accumulate before the slowdown is distinguishable from load.

\textbf{Omitting owners.} Table~\ref{tab:adv} sweeps the fraction $\alpha$ of omitting owners. With $\alpha=0.2$ \voss{} keeps success at \VossSuccAlpha\%, whereas heartbeat-only detection with otherwise identical machinery drops to \HbSuccAlpha\% (H4, supported) and
loses \HbOmitRatio$\times$ more results to omitting owners. At the same time success under omission stays near the churn-only level, which supports the claim that attested omitting owners behave like churn once detection uses progress. The \emph{robust coverage} constraint is not what
keeps success: setting $f=0$ changes success by only \FzeroSuccDelta{} percentage points (H2, inconclusive), although it lengthens P99 (Table~\ref{tab:adv}). Detection and re-dispatch carry success; robust planning buys tail latency.

\textbf{Hedging.} Always hedging cuts P99 but wastes \AlwaysWastePct\% extra attempts. Risk-proportional hedging wastes \RpWastePct\%, and its P99 is \HedgePNinetyninePct\% lower at $\alpha=0.1$, with an interval from \HedgePNinetynineLo\% to $+$\HedgePNinetynineHi\% (H-FT3, inconclusive). It is inexpensive insurance, not demonstrated to be necessary.

\subsection{RQ5: Robustness, latency and exposure}
\label{sec:res-pareto}
Robustness $f$ and the exposure weight trade latency against key exposure (E23, Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}). The aggregate front has \ParetoFrontN{} points. Exposure grows with $f$ (\ExpFZero, \ExpFOne{} and \ExpFTwo{} at $f=0$, $0.1$, $0.2$), consistent with covering any $f$ failures requiring more key-holding hosts. Aggregate sync P99 stays within
\ParetoPNinetynineLo--\ParetoPNinetynineHi\,s across configurations, so the knee at $f=\ParetoKneeF$ saves \ParetoKneeExpSaved\% of exposure against the best-latency front point for \ParetoKneePNinetynineLoss\% more P99 (H3, supported). Per tenant class the picture is less benign: low-sensitivity tenants stay at about
\ParetoLowMax\,s or below, but for high-sensitivity tenants P99 reaches \ParetoHighMax\,s in one robust configuration, while an $f=0$ configuration has the lowest exposure at nearly the best P99. The aggregate knee therefore should not be applied to every tenant class; each needs its own operating point.

\subsection{RQ6: Components that do not earn their complexity}
\label{sec:res-nopay}
\textbf{Geo-clustering.} Table~\ref{tab:geo} compares a single global scheduler against static regions, static $k$-means and dynamic clusters. Dynamic clusters have \emph{higher} P99 than the global scheduler by \HSevenEu\% (Europe), \HSevenEuNa\% (EU+NA) and \HSevenWorld\% (world) (H7, refuted at all three
populations), and hold \GeoExpRatio$\times$ the key exposure in Europe, since each of the \GeoClusters{} clusters must cover demand with its own key holders. The network share of latency falls in some populations (Table~\ref{tab:geo}), but not enough to offset the cost of splitting the pool. We attribute this to fewer hosts per cluster for
pooling warm state and keys, but did not isolate the mechanism. We found no population in which geo-clustering wins at the scale we simulate.

\textbf{Routing and caching.} Without flash crowds, nearest-region routing meets \RouteNearMet{} of deadlines (P99 \RouteNearPNinetynine\,s), whereas routing to the least-loaded cluster meets \RouteLeastMet{} (P99 \RouteLeastPNinetynine\,s) because it ignores distance. For caching (E09), cost-aware GreedyDual with peer-to-peer fetch and an HDD tier is at most
\HSixPct\% better than LRU in sync P99 and the intervals include zero (H6, inconclusive); all variants start about 98\% of invocations from a warm VM, which leaves little for the disk tiers to improve.

\textbf{Reputation and accounting.} Attested receipts are off the critical path (H10, supported): lengthening the settlement period from 10 to 300\,s or the batch from 0.2 to 5\,s changes P99 by no more than $\pm$\HTenPct\%. The stream is small, about \RecvRows{} rows/s and \RecvKBs\,KB/s at 20\,inv/s offered load; every receipt is accounted for (\RecvUnexplained{} unexplained, at most \RecvMissingMax{} missing at the end of a run). Reputation adds
little: using a Beta-decay score as a tie-breaker lowers P99 by \RepPNinetyninePct\% (weak), and credit is highly concentrated (Gini \RepGini; the top 10\% of workers earn \RepTopTen\% of credit and \RepStarved\% earn almost none), a fairness problem for an incentive that relies on credits.

\subsection{RQ7: Baselines, commercial reference and scale}
\label{sec:res-simple}
\textbf{Simple baseline.} Table~\ref{tab:simple} compares \voss{} with the simple baseline on a homogeneous, stable, benign pool, where we expected the baseline to match. It does not: \voss{} has \HElevenPct\% lower P99 (H11, refuted), which we attribute to planning's pre-warming against churn. The baseline, however, holds about half of \voss{}'s key exposure
(\voss{} is \SimpleExpRatio$\times$ higher), consistent with its placing keys on fewer hosts. Whether the latency is worth the exposure is a policy choice that the Pareto front of RQ5 lets a deployer make.

\textbf{Commercial reference.} Table~\ref{tab:comm} compares \voss{} with commercial-style references on the same workload and engine at list prices. \textbf{Scalability.} Fig.~\ref{fig:scale} and Table~\ref{tab:scale} scale the population from $10^2$ to $10^5$ workers at constant load per worker (0.01\,inv/s) in a cheaper ``scalability mode'' (coarse heartbeats, greedy planning, 2 simulated minutes). Up to $10^4$ workers success is \ScaleTenKSucc\%. At $3\times10^4$ it falls to \ScaleMidSucc\% with a decision P99 of
\ScaleMidDecide\,s, and at $10^5$ to \ScaleBigSucc\% with sync P99 \ScaleBigPNinetynine\,s and decision P99 \ScaleBigDecide\,s: decision latency grows from about 0.1\,s to tens of seconds. We did not isolate which
orchestrator or router component saturates first, and the two largest points use three seeds.

\subsection{Hypothesis summary}
\label{sec:res-score}
Of the hypotheses stated before the runs, \ScoreSupported{} are supported, \ScoreWeak{} weak, \ScoreInconclusive{} inconclusive and \ScoreRefuted{} refuted (Table~\ref{tab:score}). The refuted entries (geo-clustering, the simple baseline on homogeneous hosts, and the cost ratio of confidential VMs) and the inconclusive ones (the robust constraint for success,
the MILP over greedy planning, caching and hedging) are as informative as the supported ones.
