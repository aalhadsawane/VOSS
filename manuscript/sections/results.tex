\subsection{The limit of one scheduler}
\label{sec:res-scale}

One scheduler is limited by how many invocations it can place per second, and the limit lies far above the pools we
evaluate. We timed the scheduler's own code on one core of an \VDecCpu{} processor, inside runs with
\VDecPoolSmall{} to \VDecPoolLarge{} workers and \VDecFnLo{} to \VDecFnHi{} functions. A placement decision takes
\VDecPlaceFixedMs{}~ms plus \VDecPlacePerPairUs{}~\textmu s for every candidate pair of invocation and worker
(Table~\ref{tab:decision}). The median decision takes \VDecPlaceSmallLoMs{}--\VDecPlaceSmallHiMs{}~ms with
\VDecPoolSmall{} workers and \VDecPlaceLargeLoMs{}--\VDecPlaceLargeHiMs{}~ms with \VDecPoolLarge{}, which is two
orders of magnitude below a tenth of the median synchronous deadline (\VDecBoundLoMs{}--\VDecBoundHiMs{}~ms). The
scheduler places one invocation at a time, so its capacity is the inverse of the decision time: \VDecCapSmall{}
invocations per second with \VDecPoolSmall{} workers and \VDecCapLargeLo{}--\VDecCapLargeHi{} with
\VDecPoolLarge{}. If every worker brought 0.1 invocations per second, the smallest measured pool whose load exceeds this capacity
would have \VDecSaturatedPool{} workers. \todoitem{T40}{held-out experiments}{Share of this capacity that the
\VRunWorkers{}-worker runs use at their highest load.} Beyond that size, workers would be divided among schedulers on the provider's machines; under a holder
budget this costs holders (Section~\ref{sec:arch}), and we do not evaluate it.
