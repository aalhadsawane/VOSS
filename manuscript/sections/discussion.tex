\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the evidence supports.} Deciding ahead of demand how many workers hold each tenant's key beats reactive rules: against
the strongest single loops we built, the planner lowers P99 by \RfourDecompGreedyMed--\RfourDecompGreedyHigh\% in \RfourHtwelveMedBelow{} and \RfourHtwelveHighBelow{} of 20 paired seeds at medium and high churn, at about 2.5 times their key
exposure. Judging workers by returned work is what keeps success high when owners drop work. These are statements about
mechanisms, backed by paired intervals, and they hold across churn levels and adversary fractions.

\textbf{What it does not.} Four expected benefits did not appear. Geographic clusters were slower than one scheduler and held
several times the exposure at our scale. The exact solver was not measurably better than greedy planning. Cost-aware caching and
the reliability score change P99 by a few percent at most. A commercial-style datacenter reference is faster. Two things we
believed about the planner did not survive its own experiments. The gain is not from choosing long-lived workers: hiding
survival estimates changes P99 by under 3\%. And it is not key-release delay, which touches 0.1--0.4\% of invocations in every
system; it is spare capacity on enough key holders when demand arrives. The planner also churns keys, releasing about
\RfourRelPlanGreedyMed{} per run against \RfourRelKeepaliveTtlMed{} for a keep-alive timer, so each release serves only about \RfourAmortPlanGreedyMed{} invocations. A planner that reused
keys longer might keep the gain at lower exposure; the loss-model planner reaches lower exposure only by giving up latency and
memory, and its exposure-budget variant did not hold its budget.

\textbf{Exposure is the price of latency.} Every system on the front of Figure~\ref{fig:front} trades the two. A deployment should
let each tenant choose its point, since low-sensitivity tenants gain latency cheaply while high-sensitivity tenants may not want
the key on more machines at all.

\textbf{Reliance on attestation.} Every robustness result uses omission and timing faults only. If attestation failed, for
example through the physical attacks we excluded, adversaries could forge results and read keys, and exposure would stop being a
proxy for risk.

\textbf{Where the scheduler runs.} The planner and request-placement results do not depend on where the scheduler runs, except
through one router-to-scheduler round trip and decision latency; the scheduler-fault results are specific to volunteer
hosting. We did not run a provider-hosted variant, and expect the first to carry over and the second not to.

\subsection{Limits of this study}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulation, partly validated.} No multi-machine deployment has validated the simulator. A single-box emulation with
real microVMs (E01) agrees on success and start-up levels but not on the shape of the latency distribution, and did not exercise
the network, attestation or failover. Policies are the real code; the world (network, churn, adversaries) is a model.
\item \textbf{A placement defect, corrected late.} Our first request placement estimated waits for a slot from one cluster-wide
mean, which handicapped baselines and made the planner's lead look larger. The corrected placement is used in the planner
comparison and the baseline experiments; the experiments about VOSS alone (ablation, failure detection, geographic clusters,
scale) ran with the original placement, which changes VOSS's own P99 by about 2\%.
\item \textbf{Assumed workload and churn.} No public trace of donation sessions exists. The workload has six tenants and 12
functions on 200 workers, so the planner's limits on tenants and functions never apply, the planner is not stressed by a long
tail of rare functions, and 11 functions are idempotent by our assumption. Absolute differences are 0.3--0.5\,s of P99, with
every system under 3\,s.
\item \textbf{Idealised knowledge.} The scheduler takes start-up, trust and network costs and declared worker speeds as exact,
and we did not sweep prediction error. Calibration comes from one laptop with a hard disk, and confidential-VM costs from the
literature.
\item \textbf{Network and decision costs.} Network delay is geographic plus 20\,ms for relayed workers. Broker capacity,
reconnection and message signing are not modelled, and decision time is a placeholder formula.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Twenty seeds (ten for the exact solver and the session experiment) give wide intervals for tail
metrics, and the best single loop per setting is chosen on the test seeds, which favours the baselines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future}
The most valuable next steps are a small real testbed that validates the simulator, a many-tenant workload with a long tail of
functions, a planner that keeps keys longer, a comparison of the volunteer-hosted and provider-hosted schedulers under a load that
stresses one scheduler, and measurement of confidential-VM hosts.
