\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the evidence supports.} The central design claim holds in simulation: managing attested session state as a planned, perishable resource beats reactive policies. Planning cuts tail latency by roughly half against no planning
under churn, lifecycle-aware placement and planning are the two largest contributors in the ablation, and progress-based detection is what keeps success high when owners omit. These statements concern mechanisms and are supported by paired intervals; they
hold across churn regimes and adversary fractions in the feasibility maps rather than at one operating point.

\textbf{What it does not.} Four expected benefits did not materialize. First, geo-clustering is slower than a single scheduler and holds several times the exposure at our scale. Second, the exact MILP is not demonstrably better than the greedy planner (its point estimate at high churn is \MilpGreedyHigh\%, but the interval includes zero), so its cost is justified only by
the coverage guarantee, which we could not show matters for \emph{success}. Third, caching sophistication and reputation tie-breaking change P99 by a few percent at most, and credit under the current rules is extremely concentrated. Fourth, a commercial-style reference has lower
latency, and \voss{} is cheaper per invocation only once the fixed control plane is amortized. None of this contradicts the premise that volunteer hardware can serve latency-sensitive functions at some SLO; it narrows which components earn their complexity. A leaner \voss{} (attestation,
a greedy robust planner, a cost-based placer with hedging, progress-based detection and epoch-fenced failover, plus receipts) is the version our evidence favors.

\textbf{Exposure is the price of latency.} Planning, robustness and warm state all enlarge the set of hosts holding a tenant's key. The simple baseline has about half of \voss{}'s exposure, and the Pareto front shows that moderate robustness captures most of the benefit for aggregate latency
but not for every tenant class. A deployment should expose exposure and robustness as per-tenant policy, not as global constants.

\textbf{Substrate and population matter more than tuning.} The feasibility maps show that the choice of trust substrate and a minimum population dominate parameter tuning: on confidential-VM substrates the simple baseline can suffice at high load, and below a few hundred hosts no system is feasible.

\textbf{Reliance on attestation.} Every robustness result uses omission and timing faults only. If attestation failed, for example through the physical attacks excluded in Section~\ref{sec:threat}, adversaries could forge results and read keys, and exposure would no longer be a proxy for risk. This is why the design treats liveness
and owner notices as hints and verifies progress.

\subsection{Extension to function workflows}
\label{sec:dag}
The model of Sections~\ref{sec:overview}--\ref{sec:ft} treats invocations as independent. Many serverless applications are instead
\emph{workflows}, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) of functions in which the completion of one function triggers its successors
\cite{copik2024sebsflow}. This is outside the scheduling contribution of this paper and we do not evaluate it, but the structure is
exactly the kind of information a trust-aware scheduler can use: when a function is called, the functions in its subtree will be called
soon, so their start-up and provisioning can be overlapped with the parent's execution instead of paid in sequence. Without that
overlap every stage of a chain can pay a cold start in turn, a \emph{cascading cold start} \cite{daw2020xanadu}. Table~\ref{tab:dag}
states how each part of \voss{} would extend, and which prior ideas apply.

Two properties make workflows a natural fit. First, lead time gives the planner slack: the provisioning round trip and a snapshot restore of a child can be hidden behind
the parent, which converts a latency that the fast loop must otherwise price into an overlap. Second, keeping workflow state at the router rather than on a volunteer host
follows the progress principle of Section~\ref{sec:overview} and keeps volunteers out of the coordination path. The risk is speculation: pre-warming a child that a conditional
branch does not call wastes donor memory, and warming it on an additional host enlarges exposure, so look-ahead warming should be budgeted like hedging and restricted to
hosts that are already provisioned for the tenant. Evaluating this extension needs a workflow benchmark such as SeBS-Flow \cite{copik2024sebsflow} and is future work.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulation only.} No multi-machine deployment has validated the simulator end to end. Policies are the real code, but the world model (network, churn, adversaries) is a model. We interpret differences between designs, not absolute latencies.
\item \textbf{Calibration scope.} Calibration comes from one consumer laptop with a hard disk, and the appliance ran in host mode. Confidential-VM and DRTM parameters are literature values. E24 shows the main comparison survives a change of bundle, but a different CPU, disk or firmware could shift absolute costs.
\item \textbf{Assumed workload and churn.} No public trace of consumer donation sessions exists; churn shapes are assumed from availability studies and swept. The function catalogue follows SeBS but its sizes are assumed. The adversary model is omission and timing with a fixed menu of strategies; an adaptive adversary that learns the scheduler could do worse.
\item \textbf{The scheduler's knowledge of costs is idealised.} The scheduler uses calibrated nominal start-up, trust and network times, and takes declared host speeds and link rates as exact (Table~\ref{tab:info}), whereas the simulated world adds jitter and noise. A deployment would estimate these online. We did not sweep prediction error or staleness, so the results show the benefit of the \emph{policies} under this idealisation, not their robustness to cost misestimation.
\item \textbf{Scale.} The two largest scalability points use three seeds and a cheaper simulator mode, and a $10^5$-worker deployment may behave differently.
\item \textbf{Statistics and selection.} Ten seeds give wide intervals for tail metrics, and several comparisons are inconclusive rather than null. We ran many experiments, so a verdict on a single comparison should be read with that in mind. Specifications were frozen before the final sweep, but pilots informed which cells to include.
\item \textbf{Literature parameters.} Parameters taken from cited studies (for example confidential-VM start-up) are quoted as reported and tagged for re-verification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
Validate on a small real testbed with end-to-end measurements, which would also test the host-mode appliance assumption; measure confidential-VM hosts to replace literature parameters; shard the orchestrator tier and re-measure scalability; revisit geo-clustering at larger
populations and with shared trust across clusters; evaluate workflow-aware look-ahead warming (Section~\ref{sec:dag}); and study adaptive adversaries and credit-based incentives given the observed credit concentration.
