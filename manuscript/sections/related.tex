\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\voss{} needs six things at once: tolerance of untrusted machines, tolerance of churn, latency targets, planning of keys and
warm sandboxes, detection of gray failures, and verifiable records of work. Each line of work below covers some of them.

\textbf{Volunteer computing.} BOINC-style systems use idle machines for scientific batch
work~\cite{anderson2002seti,anderson2004boinc}. Because the machines are untrusted, they run each task several times and
accept a result when enough copies agree, and they use deadlines of hours to tolerate machines that disappear. Replication
multiplies cost, needs tasks that always give the same answer, protects results but not the confidentiality of inputs, and
works on a timescale of hours. \voss{} uses attestation for integrity and confidentiality instead, and duplicates work only
against dropped results, and only for requests at risk.

\textbf{Serverless scheduling and cold starts.} Work on cold starts decides when to keep sandboxes alive or start them
early~\cite{shahrad2020wild}, treats keep-alive as caching~\cite{fuerst2021faascache}, or makes start-up faster with lighter
sandboxes and snapshots~\cite{agache2020firecracker,oakes2018sock,ao2022faasnap,cadden2020seuss}. For placement, Sparrow
schedules short tasks by sampling a few machines~\cite{ousterhout2013sparrow}, Falcon moves a central scheduler into a
programmable switch to cut its latency~\cite{kettaneh2020falcon}, and Borg packs long-running jobs onto large
fleets~\cite{verma2015borg}. Inside one machine, ALPS learns a priority schedule for function processes from recent
traces~\cite{fu2024alps}. All of these assume trusted machines that stay up: a warm sandbox disappears only when a policy
evicts it, and there is nothing like a key whose release costs time and adds exposure. \voss{} reuses the start-up levels and
the per-request placement ideas, and adds a trust step per session and a plan that accounts for churn and exposure.

\textbf{Two-timescale placement in edge computing.} Mobile edge computing commonly splits decisions by timescale: which
services or resources to place on edge servers is decided slowly, and where each request goes is decided quickly. Farhadi et
al.\ place services and schedule requests at two timescales with an approximation guarantee~\cite{farhadi2021ton}, and Tang
et al.\ combine slow resource provisioning by Gibbs sampling with fast workload scheduling for cloud-assisted edge
computing~\cite{tang2024rmws}. \voss{} shares this structure, and Section~\ref{sec:why2} gives our reason for it. What differs
is the resource being placed: trust in the form of keys, whose cost is exposure and whose benefit is the slow path avoided;
machines that disappear with probabilities we estimate; and protection against worst-case rather than random losses. The
Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty method often used in that literature is a natural way to turn our exposure weight into a budget.

\textbf{Harvested and edge serverless.} Platforms on spare datacenter capacity~\cite{zhang2021harvest} and edge schedulers~\cite{rausch2021edge} run on machines that belong to the provider or a trusted party, so they handle announced evictions, not machines that silently stop answering, and give no secret to a party that might read it.

\textbf{Confidential serverless.} Trusted execution environments protect code from the host operating system and the
platform operator~\cite{arnautov2016scone,costan2016sgx,amd2020snp}, and recent work runs serverless functions in
confidential virtual machines and containers~\cite{wallet2025,segarra2024sesame,misono2025cvm}. These designs target
provider-managed servers. They do not deal with machines whose sessions end without warning, and they do not schedule the
establishment of trust as a resource with a cost, a lifetime and an exposure. \voss{} can run over either kind of machine.

\textbf{Trusted components and failure detection.} Small trusted components, such as a counter or log, restrict what a faulty
node can do and reduce the replication needed for Byzantine agreement~\cite{levin2009trinc,veronese2013minbft}. We use
attestation differently: it does not make agreement cheaper, it removes the need for it, because an attested machine can
stop but not lie, so a provider-run referee can replace agreement among volunteers. For detection, heartbeat detectors,
including the adaptive $\varphi$-accrual detector~\cite{hayashibara2004phi}, estimate whether a process is running from the
timing of its heartbeats. Gray failures~\cite{huang2017gray} show why that is not enough, and \voss{} instead checks whether
accepted work comes back.

\textbf{Brokers and duplicates.} MQTT~\cite{mqtt5} gives no protection against the broker itself, and MQTT-I adds end-to-end
integrity for data flows through an untrusted broker~\cite{buccafurri2024mqtti}; we sign messages end to end for the same
reason. Hedged requests reduce tail latency by duplicating slow work~\cite{dean2013tail}; \voss{} duplicates in proportion to
estimated risk, on a machine in a different city or provider, within a budget. For protection against losing $f$ machines we
use the budgeted robust optimisation of Bertsimas and Sim~\cite{bertsimas2004price}, which answers ``is demand still covered
if any $f$ machines fail together?'' without a probability model of the adversary.
