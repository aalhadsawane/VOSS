\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most serverless invocations are short: in production traces a large share of functions run for well under a second
\cite{shahrad2020wild}, and for such functions network distance and cold starts, not computation, dominate latency. Idle
consumer machines are abundant, close to users, and owned by nobody who has to be paid in capital expenditure. Volunteer
computing has harvested them for two decades \cite{anderson2002seti,anderson2004boinc}, but only for delay-tolerant batch
work whose deadlines are hours or days. Serving \emph{latency-sensitive} functions from volunteer hardware raises two
problems that batch systems avoid. The machines are \emph{unreliable}: owners reboot, unplug and reclaim them at will, so
every warm sandbox is perishable. And they are \emph{untrusted}: an owner can read a tenant's code and data, return
forged results, or defect at the worst moment.

Classical volunteer systems answer the second problem with redundancy and voting, which triples cost and does not apply to
functions with side effects. Our starting point is different. If tenant code and the scheduler's own code run only inside a
measured, owner-locked appliance image whose TPM-backed attestation a verifier checks, a hostile owner can no longer read,
forge or tamper; what remains is \emph{omission and timing}: power off, drop, delay, throttle. Attestation thus reduces
Byzantine owners to the fault class that churn already forces a volunteer system to tolerate
\cite{levin2009trinc,veronese2013minbft}. We make this reduction the foundation of the design rather than a footnote.

The reduction changes what scheduling is about. \emph{Trust} becomes a resource with three properties.
It is costly to establish: booting into the appliance, attesting, and releasing a tenant's key to a host all take time.
It is perishable: all of it vanishes when the owner ends the donation session. And it is exposing: every additional host
that holds a tenant's key widens that tenant's attack surface. Placing an invocation on a host that is not yet
trusted for the tenant is therefore not a free choice, and neither is asking more hosts to hold the key.

We present \voss{} (volunteer-oriented serverless scheduling), a scheduler built around this view. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A threat-reduction argument and substrate model} (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}): attestation maps malicious owners to
omission/timing faults, and a data-driven substrate profile (TPM appliance, confidential VMs, DRTM, unattested) makes
trust establishment, perishability, warm-state ladders and assurance explicit.
\item \textbf{Two-timescale, trust-aware scheduling} (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}): a slow robust mixed-integer program plans
\emph{where trust and warm state live}, with coverage that survives any $f$ defecting hosts, memory and exposure costs and
failure-domain caps; a fast placer decides \emph{where each invocation runs} from a cost model over the same plan, with
risk-proportional hedging.
\item \textbf{Fault tolerance that treats liveness as a hint} (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}): progress-based detection, scheduler
succession without volunteer consensus, and attested receipts that make accounting verifiable without a blockchain.
\item \textbf{A deterministic, calibrated simulator and a mechanical evaluation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}): paired
comparisons over 10 seeds, a viability map over churn, adversaries, substrates, load and population, a latency--exposure--robustness
Pareto analysis, and a scorecard in which each hypothesis has a stated verdict rule, so that regimes where baselines win are
reported by construction.
\item \textbf{Measurements} from a TPM-equipped machine that parameterize attestation, boot and snapshot costs
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\end{enumerate}

Our results (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) are mixed, and we report them as such. Planning trust and warm state is the largest
lever: removing it costs \AblPlanningP\,s of sync P99, and removing lifecycle-aware placement costs \AblLifecycleP\,s. Robust
planning buys tail latency at the price of key exposure. Progress-based detection is decisive against omitting owners and
black-holed schedulers. But geo-clustering, a design pillar of earlier volunteer-scheduling proposals, \emph{loses} to one
global scheduler at our scale; reputation, tiered caches and the integer program over a greedy planner each give little
or nothing measurable; a simple baseline holds about half of \voss{}'s key exposure; a commercial-style reference cloud has lower P99; and
the system degrades at \ScaleBigSucc\% success at $10^5$ workers.

\emph{Scope and honesty.} All results come from simulation. The simulator is calibrated with measurements from one
TPM-equipped consumer laptop, not from the target node, and it has not been validated end to end against a real
multi-machine testbed (Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}). Confidential-VM substrates use literature parameters. We therefore read our
numbers as evidence about \emph{mechanisms and regimes}, not as performance predictions.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work. Sections~\ref{sec:model} and
\ref{sec:design} describe the model and the design. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the simulator, calibration and
protocol, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss limits and conclude.
