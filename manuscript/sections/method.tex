\section{Evaluation Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulator}
All results come from a deterministic discrete-event simulator that runs the \emph{same policy code} as the scheduler.
Policies (planner, placer, router, hedger, detector) are pure functions of immutable views; the layered code base enforces
by static import checks that a policy cannot import the simulated world, so it cannot read future state. Every
cross-node interaction is a typed message delivered after a delay derived from great-circle distance between about 100 metro
areas plus a per-hop overhead. Nothing is free: network transfer, disk-tier reads, CPU contention, TPM quotes,
verification, key release and the decision latency of planner, placer, router and orchestrator all consume simulated time.
All randomness comes from named, seeded streams, so a run is a function of its scenario and seed; a manifest records the
scenario and calibration hashes and \texttt{voss repro} regenerates result hashes.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Population and churn.}
Workers are drawn from three tiers (nightly, idle-hours and dedicated) with heterogeneous hardware, home metros and
substrates. Donation sessions follow lognormal, Weibull or nightly-schedule length distributions; a share of departures is
graceful (with a drain notice) and the rest abrupt; correlated shocks remove a fraction of a city at once. Three churn
regimes (low, medium, high) plus burst and correlated variants parameterize these. No public consumer-donation trace
exists, so the regime parameters are tagged \emph{assumed}; their shapes follow volunteer-availability studies
\cite{javadi2013fta}, and E14 sweeps them.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Workload.}
The function catalogue has 12 functions modeled on SeBS benchmarks \cite{copik2021sebs} with sync, async and filler SLO
classes. Tenants have a sensitivity weight $\sigma_t$. Arrivals follow a Markov-modulated Poisson process (4$\times$ bursts) with Zipf-skewed function popularity and a diurnal
modulation; a flash-crowd regime multiplies the rate. A sync invocation's deadline is $k$ times its warm
latency ($k=5$ for the sync functions). Unless stated, runs use 15 simulated minutes, a 20\,inv/s offered load and
200--600 workers (as stated per experiment); the grids for E18 and E20 differ and are described with their results.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Adversaries.}
A fraction $\alpha$ of attested workers is \emph{omitting}: it follows the protocol, keeps heartbeats flowing, and silently
drops, stalls or throttles work (black-hole, slow, and colluding-defection variants). By the threat-reduction argument
(Sec.~\ref{sec:threat}) no adversary on an attested substrate forges results or reads secrets in the attested substrates; the \texttt{none}
substrate, used for unattested baselines, admits both.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calib}
Seed parameters in the specification are literature values or assumptions, tagged as such and never silently promoted.
Measured values come only from calibration bundles. We measured a bundle on one consumer machine, an \CalCpu{} laptop with a
discrete TPM~2.0 and a hard disk, which stands in for the target node. The harness reboots the machine into the
appliance and records wall-clock timestamps (resolution about 1\,s); microbenchmarks cover TPM quotes, hashing,
Firecracker boot and snapshots. Table~\ref{tab:calib} compares the measured values with the placeholders used
before measurement. The most consequential differences are slower reboot-to-kernel (\CalReboot\,s against \CalRebootPh\,s),
a much slower kernel-to-agent phase (\CalUserspace\,s) and snapshot creation on a hard disk (\CalSnapCreate\,s for 512\,MB).
Reboot to a fully attested worker takes \CalAttested\,s (median of \CalAttestedN{} boots). Firecracker boot with a warm

\subsection{Protocol}
Each experiment is a frozen specification of base scenario, systems and a grid; \emph{every} cell is run with seeds 1--10
(three seeds for the two largest scalability points, whose runs take up to 20 wall-clock minutes and 9\,GB). All
comparisons are \emph{paired}: for a metric $m$, system $A$ and baseline $B$, we form $\Delta_s = m_A(s) - m_B(s)$ per seed
$s$ within the same scenario cell and report the mean with a 95\% percentile-bootstrap interval of the paired differences
\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. Relative changes are $\Delta / m_B$ with $m_B$ the baseline mean; interval ends can therefore exceed 100\%. Tables report mean $\pm$ the half-width of the bootstrap interval of the mean.

Each hypothesis is stated before the run together with a verdict rule applied mechanically to the intervals:
\emph{supported} when the interval excludes zero in the predicted direction and the mean effect is at least a stated
relative margin, \emph{weak} when it excludes zero but the effect is below the margin, \emph{inconclusive} when it includes zero, and \emph{refuted} when it excludes
zero in the opposite direction. The scorecard (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-score}) lists all of them. Specifications were frozen before the final sweep, and the deviations from the pilots are recorded in a decision log. The manuscript's numbers,
tables and several sentence-level claims are produced by a script that reads the result tables and \emph{fails} if a result stops
supporting the sentence it backs.

\subsection{Metrics and viability}
We report success (fraction of invocations completed), the deadline-met fraction, sync P50/P99 end-to-end latency,
\emph{waste} (extra accepted attempts per successful invocation: retries, hedges and adopted re-dispatches),
\emph{donor memory} (GB held warm), and \emph{key-holder exposure}
$E=\sum_t\sigma_t\sum_j(1-a_j)\,y_{tj}$ at the end of the run (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}). Exposure is comparable only within
a substrate mix, because it weights each key-holding host by $(1-a_j)$, the residual lack of assurance of its substrate.

A system is \emph{viable} in a scenario cell if the lower 95\% bound of its success is at least $0.99$ and either the lower
bound of the deadline-met fraction is at least the stated target or its sync P99 is below an absolute target (2, 3 or 5\,s).
E20 sweeps three two-dimensional panels (churn $\times$ adversary fraction; substrate $\times$ load; population $\times$ load) at
300 workers, 10 simulated minutes, comparing \voss{}, the strongest simple baseline, and the same baseline without
attestation. The viability map is thus a statement about \emph{where} each design works, not an average.

\subsection{Baselines}
Baselines use the same simulator, candidate sets and cost matrix as \voss{} and differ only in the registered policy.
\emph{No planning} (never pre-warms), \emph{keep-alive TTL} (an idle timer), \emph{greedy planner} (the same objective
solved by a heuristic), \emph{heartbeat-only} detection, \emph{non-robust} planning ($f=0$), and the \emph{simple baseline}:
least-loaded placement on the lowest-latency attested worker, no planning and no hedging. Commercial-style references
(\emph{Lambda-like}, \emph{Azure-like}) are always-on datacenter hosts in the router metros with per-function keep-alive
(10 and 20\,min) and nearest-region routing, priced from list prices; they run on the same engine so that differences are
due to the policy and substrate, not the simulator.
